## Claim extraction

### C-T1.6-001 / S-0002 / R4, R6

**Exact proposed claim:** In the June-September 2013 experiments, the authors used objects from the Alexa top-200 home pages, consolidated embedded objects on their own server, repeated experiments five times and reported the median. The §4 transfer experiment used custom clients without browser dependencies/computation; its PLT is not a live-browser load-event measurement.

**Original passages:** §4.1, PDF p. 5: “we download the home pages of the Alexa top 200 websites to our own server”; “we serve all embedded objects from the same server including those that are dynamically generated by JavaScript.” The same page states “We repeat our experiments five times and present the median” and explicitly says “we do not work with browsers.” PLT is elapsed time from first object requested to last object received, not the W3C load event.

**Design/context:** Controlled HTTP versus SPDY/3 object transfers; synthetic object sets and historically popular page-derived sets. Unit is a page/object-set transfer under a network/protocol configuration, with repeated executions, not an independent human user. Dummynet varies the network on a campus LAN. Apache 2.2.2/mod_spdy 0.9.3.3-386, Ubuntu 12.04/Linux 3.7.5, Cubic; SSL and gzip disabled; HTTP allows six parallel TCP connections. Cache/lifecycle equivalence to a normal browser is not established. Allocation is a parameter sweep; randomized order/blocking is not established by the checked passage. Median aggregation addresses random loss but is not a confidence interval or a precision-based repetition rule. Failures/missing pages are not quantified in this extraction.

**Support:** **confirmed** for this bounded historical method, not for a claim of 200 live-browser validations. **Confidence: higher** for the directly documented method; **limited** for transfer to contemporary profiler evaluation.

### C-T1.6-002 / S-0002 / R3, R6

**Exact proposed claim:** SPDY's benefits in this study depend on network/workload conditions and on whether dependency and computation delays are retained; omitting these delays can exaggerate the benefit. No universal SPDY speedup follows.

**Original passages:** Abstract, PDF p. 2: dependencies and computation can overwhelm benefits, “reducing the improvements with SPDY to 7% for our lower bandwidth and higher RTT scenarios.” §5.2, PDF p. 10: dependencies and computation “reduce and can easily nullify the benefits of SPDY.” §4.3, PDF p. 7 reports benefit for 70% of the tested pages consistently across network conditions in the dependency-free experiment; this is not a live-browser success rate.

**Comparator/metrics/analysis:** SPDY PLT divided by HTTP PLT, with network settings controlled; §5 introduces Epload, not a browser-per-condition live experiment. §5.1, PDF p. 9 records WProf dependency graphs and replaces computation with recorded-duration waits while making real network requests. Layout/painting dependencies are excluded there because they do not occur deterministically and significantly in the authors' setup. §5.2 compares emulated dependencies and scaled computation with the dependency-free result. Abstract 7% remains scenario-qualified, not a pooled effect or recommended target. Packet loss can reverse the protocol ranking (abstract); this contrary result matters as much as benefits. Neither five medians nor modeled computation prove unbiased contemporary browser behavior.

**Support:** **qualified**: conditional benefit is supported, an unconditional or modern-browser effect is not. **Confidence: limited** for quantitative transfer because this is a historical protocol/emulation design.
